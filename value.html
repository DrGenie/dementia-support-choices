5. Compare with another programme

How the numbers are calculated

Benefit to participants

For each person offered the programme, the choice model gives the money value of having the programme available compared with no programme, net of the fee (the expected consumer surplus, from the "logsum" of the choice probabilities). It is measured per session, because the fee and willingness to pay in the study were per session. The yearly benefit is this value multiplied by the number of people offered and the number of sessions in a year, plus the fees participants pay.

Costs, fees and net benefit

The running cost is the provider cost per participant per session multiplied by the expected number of participants and the number of sessions in a year. Fees are paid by participants to the provider, so they appear on both sides and cancel out of the net benefit to society. The benefit-cost ratio is total benefit to participants (including fees) divided by the running cost.

Break-even running cost

The break-even running cost is the provider cost per participant per session at which benefits equal costs. If your programme costs less than this to run, it passes the benefit-cost test. It needs no cost information, so it is a useful first check.

Traffic lights

Green: benefits exceed costs and the 95% interval for the benefit-cost ratio is above 1. Amber: the interval includes 1, so the result is uncertain. Red: costs exceed benefits even at the upper end of the interval.

What is not included

The benefit is what respondents valued about the programme when choosing for a loved one. Health effects that people were not aware of, effects on carers' paid work, and savings to other services are not counted separately. The analysis covers one year, assumes participants attend every session, and assumes everyone offered the programme makes one decision about it. Values from a survey can be higher than what people would pay in practice.

About this website

Dementia Support Choices presents the results of a discrete choice experiment with 264 Australian adults, carried out by researchers at the University of Newcastle. It gives information from research. It is not medical advice and does not replace advice from health professionals, aged care assessors or service providers.
