How the study was done

We used a discrete choice experiment, a survey method widely used in health economics to measure what people value. Respondents were asked to imagine they were looking for a programme to help a loved one living with dementia, and that an informal carer could attend with them. Each respondent saw eight choice tasks. Each task showed two programmes, A and B, described by seven features.

Respondents first chose the better of the two programmes, then answered: "If you have the option not to choose either of these programs, will your choice remain the same?" This dual-response format records both which programme is preferred and whether it would be used at all.

The attributes and levels were based on a review of studies of dementia support programmes and refined through expert consultation. The 24 choice tasks came from a D-efficient design created in Ngene and were split into three blocks of eight; the order of tasks was randomised. Combinations that do not occur in practice were excluded, for example group programmes held in a person's home. The survey was piloted with 29 respondents before the main data collection.

Who took part

264 adults completed the survey between 24 March and 7 April 2025. They were recruited through a national online panel with quotas for age, gender and location, and were aged 18 to 74 (mean 43.8 years). The sample was 51.5% female, 77.7% were born in Australia, and state and territory shares were within about two percentage points of the 2021 Census. The sample was more educated than the population: 43.9% held a university degree.

Half (50.8%) knew or had known someone with dementia; of these, 38.1% reported a close family member and 53.8% daily or weekly contact. One in five (20.8%) had caring responsibilities for an adult with a chronic condition. The median time to complete the survey was 8.8 minutes. No respondent was excluded; 17 data-quality checks were used in sensitivity analyses instead, and none changed the direction of any significant result.

Main results

What each feature is worth

Willingness to pay is the change in cost per session that would leave people equally happy with the programme after a change in one feature, compared with the reference level. Positive values are features people would pay for; negative values are features they would need to be compensated for.

Models used on this website

All calculations run in your browser from one file of model estimates, model.json, exported from the MATLAB analysis. For each model the file contains the estimates and 1,000 draws from their sampling distribution (Krinsky and Robb method), which are used for the 95% intervals.

  • Multinomial logit. The benchmark model of the choice between Program A, Program B and neither. Used for willingness to pay, relative importance and the simpler take-up figures.
  • Mixed logit with a random constant for choosing neither. The main model for take-up. It lets people differ in how much they value each feature and in their general willingness to use any programme. Population take-up is averaged over 1,000 simulated people.
  • Piecewise cost model. Allows the effect of cost to differ between A$0 and A$5, A$5 and A$20, and A$20 and A$50.
  • Sequential dual-response model. Models the two answers to each task separately; used for the share who would keep a programme once chosen.
  • Lived-experience model. Lets the value of carer inclusion depend on caring responsibilities, a close family member with dementia and daily or weekly contact.
  • Latent class model. Divides respondents into two groups with different preferences.

Take-up is the probability of choosing a programme rather than no programme when it is offered on its own. Price elasticity is the percentage change in take-up for a 1% increase in the fee.

Limitations

  • Respondents were recruited from an online panel and were more educated than the Australian population. With 264 respondents, results for small subgroups are uncertain.
  • Respondents chose for a hypothetical loved one. Most were not carers or people living with dementia, whose preferences may differ.
  • Take-up is stated acceptance in a survey, not attendance. People may be less sensitive to price in a survey than in real life.
  • Online programmes were always delivered at home, so the effect of travel cannot be separated from delivery mode for online programmes.
  • Provider costs, capacity and waiting lists are not part of the study.

Check against the published analysis

Each time this page loads, it recalculates the eight example programmes from the published analysis and compares the results with the values from MATLAB. Small differences in the mixed logit column come from simulation.

About this website

Dementia Support Choices presents the results of a discrete choice experiment with 264 Australian adults, carried out by researchers at the University of Newcastle. It gives information from research. It is not medical advice and does not replace advice from health professionals, aged care assessors or service providers.
