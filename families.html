Choosing support for someone living with dementia

Support programmes can help people living with dementia stay connected and give carers a break and a place to share. This page explains the main options, helps you work out what matters most to you, and gives you questions to ask before you decide.

Types of support programmes

Community support programmes sit alongside medical care and aged care services. They are usually run by dementia organisations, local councils, community health services and aged care providers. The names vary, but most fit one of these types.

Peer support

Groups where people living with dementia, or carers, meet others in a similar situation to share experiences and practical tips. Groups are often led by a trained facilitator or a volunteer with lived experience.

Community engagement

Social groups, day programmes, outings, music, art and exercise groups, and outreach activities. The aim is to stay active and connected with the community.

Psychological counselling

One-to-one or family sessions with a counsellor or psychologist to talk through the diagnosis, changes in relationships, grief, stress and planning ahead.

Virtual reality programmes

Sessions using a headset to explore familiar places, nature scenes or activities. These are newer and less widely available.

How programmes can differ

  • Support for the carer. Some programmes are designed for the person with dementia only. Others also support the family member or friend who cares for them, for example by including them in sessions or offering carer education.
  • How you take part. In person, online by phone or video, or a mix of both.
  • How often and how long. From a monthly meeting to daily sessions, and from 30 minutes to several hours.
  • Where. At home, in the local area, or further away in the wider community.
  • Cost. Many programmes are free. Others charge a fee per session, or are paid for through a government-funded aged care service after an assessment.

What matters to you

Answer the questions below for the person you are looking for support for. There are no right answers. When you finish, you will get a summary of your priorities and a list of questions to ask providers. You can print it or save it as a PDF to take to appointments.

Your answers stay in this browser window. They are not sent or stored anywhere, and they disappear when you close the page.

Questions to ask a provider

These questions are useful whatever your priorities. Your summary above adds questions based on your answers.

  • Who is the programme for: the person with dementia, the carer, or both together?
  • What happens in a typical session, and who runs it? What training do staff or volunteers have in dementia care?
  • How often does it run, how long is each session, and can we start with a trial session?
  • Where is it held? Is transport provided or is there parking and public transport nearby?
  • Can we take part online if travel is difficult on some days?
  • What does it cost? Is there a fee waiver, and can it be paid for through an aged care service?
  • What happens if needs change as dementia progresses?
  • Is there support for people from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds, or for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people?

About this website

Dementia Support Choices presents the results of a discrete choice experiment with 264 Australian adults, carried out by researchers at the University of Newcastle. It gives information from research. It is not medical advice and does not replace advice from health professionals, aged care assessors or service providers.
